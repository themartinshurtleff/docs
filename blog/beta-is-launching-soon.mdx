# Beta Is Launching Soon

We are days away from launching the wider beta.

I am still trying to make that sound normal, because it feels anything but normal. This was some fucking development. The kind that looks ugly up close. Obsessive, messy, and sometimes the first thing I thought about when I woke up.

The odds were against us from the start. That sounds dramatic, but it is true. It was a tiny team with a very large target: build a real crypto orderflow terminal with heatmaps, footprint charts, liquidation data, open interest, multi-exchange aggregation, trading, layouts, docs, accounts, payments, all of it.

And somehow, it kept moving.

## Where It Started

TradeNet started with a simple idea.

I wanted a tool that I would actually use. One that could hold up when the market got fast and keep the whole move on one screen.

I wanted something me and my friends would genuinely keep open while trading.

So my co-founder, Constantine, and I started by asking people what they actually wanted. Friends, traders, people who stare at candles too much and know exactly what annoys them. What would you want to see? What would save you time? What would make you trust a setup more? What do you keep duct taping together with three websites and a Discord alert?

That is where the terminal started.

It came from actual conversations with people we cared about building for.

## What It Turned Into

At first it looked like a frontend problem. Pretty quickly, it became everything else too.

The heatmaps alone turned into a world of their own. Liquidation data had to be useful, fast, and readable. Open interest had to make sense across venues. Aggregated volume and footprint data had to feel simple on the screen even though getting it there was anything but simple. Trading, accounts, access, payments, the website, and docs all became part of the same problem.

Every feature pulled another system behind it.

The terminal became a full stack project before we fully realized it. Every feature needed more than a button and a chart. It needed real data, fast rendering, stable infrastructure, clean UX, and enough behind-the-scenes work to make the whole thing feel simple when a trader actually opens it.

That part taught me a lot. The kind of knowledge tutorials never really give you. Breaking something, staring at logs, realizing your original assumption was wrong, and fixing the system anyway.

## The Ugly Parts

Heatmaps crashed.

Bar statistic bugs made me lose sleep for three weeks. I mean that literally. I would wake up and immediately think about timestamp bucketing, partial loads, and why a number was zero when it should have been there.

Server issues showed up. Load issues showed up. Weird performance problems only appeared when enough things were happening at once, which is exactly the worst time to find them.

There was a point during beta testing where a Chinese hacker planted a crypto miner on one of our servers and throttled CPU usage to 100%. That was a fun one. Nothing quite like trying to test your own product and realizing the machine is busy mining someone else's coin.

About three months in, I got hyperthyroidism from grinding too much and eating too little. That was probably the clearest signal that I was doing this wrong. I had convinced myself that pushing harder was always the answer, and my body finally called bullshit.

Building TradeNet taught me software development. It also taught me deployment, security, product design, support, and patience. But honestly, more than anything, it taught me that work life balance matters in a very real way.

It is survival.

## The People Who Got It Here

My dad had a lot to do with this too.

He taught me a lot about work. More importantly, he kept drilling this slightly insane belief into my head that I could actually do it. At some points that belief felt ridiculous. Like actual delusion. But I think you need a little of that to build something that keeps resisting you.

Constantine was just as necessary.

He was there through every part of it. Looking over feature implementations, stress testing, co-creating the UI with me, pushing back when something needed to be better, and refusing to give up when it would have been very easy to call the whole thing too much.

TradeNet has never been just mine.

## Where We Are Now

Now we are almost there.

Beta is launching soon. The website is getting cleaned up. The docs are live. The terminal is being prepared for more actual users after a long stretch of private testing and late-night debugging sessions. The focus now is simple: open access carefully, keep the experience honest, and make sure the product can handle real traders using it.

I am nervous. Very nervous.

But I am also happy. Proud, even if that word feels strange to write. This took more out of me than I expected, but it also gave me more than I expected.

We built something real. Rough in places, unfinished in others, but real.

And if we do this right, TradeNet can become the kind of tool that changes how crypto traders read the market. That has always been the point. A better terminal for people who actually care about orderflow.

We just want to change the world a little.

Or maybe a lot. We will see.
